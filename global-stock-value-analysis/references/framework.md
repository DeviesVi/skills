# Value Investing Framework

## Research Checklist

Business quality:
- understandable business model and revenue drivers
- circle of competence: whether the analyst can understand the business, accounting, and industry well enough to estimate value
- durable competitive advantage or switching cost
- identified moat source: intangible assets, switching costs, network effects, cost advantage, or efficient scale
- industry structure and pricing power
- customer concentration and supplier dependence
- regulatory, geopolitical, and technology disruption risk
- business model quality in Duan Yongping terms: whether the company can consistently earn good money without heroic assumptions

Financial quality:
- revenue and earnings growth over 3-10 years
- gross, operating, and net margin trend
- ROE, ROIC, ROA, and reinvestment runway
- operating cash flow, free cash flow conversion, and owner earnings after maintenance capex
- leverage, interest coverage, liquidity, and debt maturity
- dilution, share count trend, buybacks, dividends
- management quality and capital allocation record
- management culture, incentives, integrity, and treatment of minority shareholders

Valuation:
- P/E, forward P/E, P/B, P/S, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, FCF yield
- historical range versus current multiple
- current P/E percentile versus the selected 5/10/20-year history, with the selected horizon and reason stated
- peer comparison using similar business models and growth profiles
- DCF for cash-generative businesses
- DDM for stable dividend businesses
- asset-based valuation for financials, real estate, or holding companies when appropriate
- scenario analysis using bear/base/bull assumptions

Risk and red flags:
- earnings growth without cash flow support
- rising receivables/inventory faster than sales
- large related-party transactions
- frequent accounting changes or restatements
- weak audit/governance signals
- high refinancing need during tight credit conditions
- aggressive capitalized costs or non-GAAP adjustments
- too much reliance on leverage, refinancing, short-term price momentum, or multiple expansion
- thesis requires the analyst to understand a technology, policy, accounting treatment, or industry dynamic that is outside the circle of competence

## Scoring Rubric

Use a 0-5 score for each category when the user wants ranking or screening:

| Category | Weight | What 5 means |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Business quality | 25% | durable moat, strong industry position, low fragility |
| Financial quality | 25% | high ROIC/ROE, strong cash conversion, clean balance sheet |
| Growth durability | 15% | long runway with credible reinvestment opportunities |
| Valuation | 20% | attractive versus normalized fundamentals and peers |
| Risk control | 15% | few red flags, resilient downside, good governance |

Final score = weighted average. Do not let a cheap valuation override severe financial or governance risk without explicitly flagging it.

Apply a hard cap when needed:
- If circle of competence is "too hard", cap the final score at 3 unless the user only wants exploratory research.
- If governance or accounting quality is poor, cap the final score at 3 even when valuation is statistically cheap.
- If leverage creates plausible permanent impairment, cap the final score at 3.5 unless downside protection is exceptional.

## Valuation Discipline

Use normalized fundamentals when current earnings are cyclical, temporarily depressed, or temporarily inflated.

Common methods:
- DCF: best for companies with visible cash generation.
- Owner earnings: useful for mature companies with recurring maintenance capex.
- Relative valuation: useful as a cross-check, not the sole conclusion.
- P/E percentile: useful for judging where today's market multiple sits versus the company's own history, but only after checking whether earnings are positive, representative, and comparable.
- Sum-of-the-parts: useful for conglomerates, platforms with separable segments, or holding companies.
- Book value or tangible book value: useful for banks, insurers, and asset-heavy financials.

Always state the valuation anchor:
- earnings power
- free cash flow
- book value
- dividends
- asset value
- strategic optionality

## P/E Percentile Discipline

When P/E is economically meaningful, include 5-year, 10-year, or 20-year P/E percentile data in the valuation section. Select one primary horizon and explain the reason. Add other horizons only when they materially change the interpretation.

Horizon selection:
- Use 5 years for newly listed companies, businesses whose model or accounting base changed materially, firms after major restructuring/M&A, or sectors where older history is not comparable.
- Use 10 years as the default for established companies with at least one normal business cycle and reasonably comparable earnings data.
- Use 20 years for mature, long-listed businesses where multiple cycles are relevant and the business mix/accounting regime remains comparable enough.

Interpretation rules:
- Low percentile means statistically cheap versus the company's own history, not automatically undervalued.
- High percentile means statistically expensive versus history, not automatically a sell if growth, margins, or capital returns structurally improved.
- Exclude material one-off or non-operating distortions before calculating or interpreting the percentile. Typical examples include tax provisions or tax benefits, impairments, litigation costs, restructuring charges/gains, government subsidies, asset disposal gains/losses, and accounting-policy changes.
- When using an adjusted or normalized P/E percentile, tell the user what was adjusted and whether the adjustment is sourced, estimated, or approximate.
- For cyclical, loss-making, financial, commodity, or one-off-heavy companies, downweight P/E percentile and prefer normalized earnings, book value, EV/EBITDA, or asset-based methods as appropriate.
- If percentile data is unavailable, stale, or computed from inconsistent sources, label it unavailable and avoid substituting an unsupported guess.

## Thesis Update Rules

When new information appears, ask:
- Does it change normalized revenue, margin, reinvestment runway, or terminal value?
- Does it change balance-sheet risk or probability of dilution?
- Does it reveal management quality or governance problems?
- Does it change the competitive advantage or industry structure?
- Does it create a time-bound catalyst without changing intrinsic value?

If the answer is no, classify the item as sentiment/noise or a monitoring item.

Before finalizing, invert the thesis:
- What has to go wrong for intrinsic value to be materially lower?
- Is that failure mode already visible in operations, incentives, accounting, or industry structure?
- Would a long-term owner still want the whole business at this price?
