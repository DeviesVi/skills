# Investment Philosophy Gates

Use this reference when a user asks for value-investing analysis, long-term ownership quality, moat durability, management quality, or a Buffett/Munger/Duan Yongping style view.

## Source Lens

This framework translates public value-investing principles into repeatable analysis checks:
- Warren Buffett / Berkshire Hathaway: business ownership, intrinsic value, owner earnings, durable competitive advantages, capable and honest managers, and disciplined capital allocation.
- Charlie Munger: circle of competence, inversion, opportunity cost, mental models, incentives, and avoiding obvious stupidity.
- Duan Yongping: buying stocks is buying companies; focus on business model, corporate culture, and price; avoid businesses that are hard to understand; avoid leverage and short-term speculation.

## Buffett Gates

Before valuation, ask:
- Is the business understandable enough to estimate normalized earnings power?
- Does it have durable competitive advantages, not just temporary growth?
- Does it earn high returns on incremental capital, or does growth require too much reinvestment?
- Does management allocate capital like owners: reinvest, buy back, pay dividends, or hold cash based on expected return?
- Are reported earnings close to owner earnings?
- Would the conclusion still make sense if the market closed for several years?

Owner earnings approximation:

```text
Owner earnings = net income
  + depreciation, depletion, amortization, and other non-cash charges
  - maintenance capital expenditure needed to preserve competitive position and unit volume
  +/- working-capital adjustments when structurally necessary
```

Do not treat EBITDA or operating cash flow as owner earnings when maintenance capex, stock-based compensation, lease obligations, or working-capital needs are material.

## Munger Gates

Use inversion before the final conclusion:
- What would make this investment fail permanently?
- What incentives could make management behave against minority shareholders?
- What accounting choices could make earnings look better than economics?
- What competitive force could erode the moat faster than the model assumes?
- What second-order effects could follow from regulation, technology change, or capital-cycle behavior?
- What is the opportunity cost versus the best alternative on the watchlist?

Use a "too hard" label when the business cannot be understood with available data. Being uncertain is acceptable; pretending precision is not.

## Duan Yongping Gates

Use "right business, right people, right price" as a compact final test:

| Gate | What to examine | Pass signal | Fail signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Right business | Business model, customer value, repeatability, long-term demand | Can keep earning good money over time | Fragile demand, weak differentiation, unclear cash flows |
| Right people | Founder/management culture, integrity, capital allocation, treatment of stakeholders | Consistent words/actions, owner mindset, long-term culture | Promotional behavior, related-party abuse, short-term financial engineering |
| Right price | Intrinsic value range, downside case, margin of safety | Attractive return even with conservative assumptions | Depends on perfect growth or multiple expansion |

Duan-style analysis should explicitly separate "cheap" from "understandable and valuable." A low multiple alone is not a margin of safety if the business quality or future cash flow is unclear.

## Moat Sources

When claiming a moat, identify the source rather than using the word loosely:
- intangible assets: brand, patents, regulatory licenses, reputation
- switching costs: workflow lock-in, data migration cost, customer habit, mission-critical use
- network effects: each new user/supplier/customer improves the product for others
- cost advantage: scale, route density, procurement, process advantage, resource access
- efficient scale: a market is too small or local for rational new entrants

Then test durability:
- Can competitors copy it with enough capital?
- Does technology reduce the advantage?
- Are customers gaining bargaining power?
- Is regulation strengthening or weakening the moat?
- Is growth expanding the moat or stretching it?

## Output Additions

When appropriate, add a compact philosophy block:

| Gate | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Circle of competence | Understandable / partially understandable / too hard |
| Right business | Pass / mixed / fail |
| Right people | Pass / mixed / fail |
| Right price | Attractive / watch / expensive |
| Owner earnings quality | Strong / mixed / weak |
| Inversion risk | Main way the thesis could fail |

Do not force every report to be long. For quick notes, include only the gates that change the conclusion.

## Further Reading

- Berkshire Hathaway 1986 shareholder letter: owner earnings and maintenance capital expenditure.
- Berkshire Hathaway 1996 shareholder letter: circle of competence and selected-business evaluation.
- Poor Charlie's Almanack: Munger's mental models, inversion, incentives, and psychology of human misjudgment.
- Morningstar Economic Moat methodology: moat sources including intangible assets, switching costs, network effect, cost advantage, and efficient scale.
- Public Duan Yongping interviews and Q&A: buying stocks as buying companies, understanding business models, corporate culture, and future cash flow.
